## 1. The problem

### 1.1 Continuum fluids and singularity

The Navier–Stokes equations apply Newton’s second law to a continuum fluid parcel. They underwrite aircraft design, weather codes, and blood-flow models. The Clay Millennium question asks whether smooth three-dimensional incompressible motion can develop a **singularity** — speeds unbounded in finite time — despite viscosity’s smoothing tendency. A real fluid cannot move infinitely fast; such a singularity would mark a breakdown of the continuum model.

### 1.2 Clay alternatives (forced vs unforced)

Clay’s official formulation admits several resolving statements. Roughly:

| Label | Shape | Force |
|-------|--------|-------|
| **A / B** | Global smoothness / regularity statements experts often picture as “the” problem | **Unforced** (or force-free idealization) |
| **C / D** | Finite-time blowup constructions | **Smooth external force allowed** |

OpenAI’s public writeup ([On the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize Problem](https://openai.com/index/navier-stokes-solution/), 2026-09-08) claims an initially smooth fluid at rest, driven by a **smooth force**, develops a singularity in finite time with energy remaining finite — establishing **C** and **D**. The pictured solution is a vortex that spirals inward and elongates “like spaghetti.”

Scientific American coverage (e.g. Howlett, 2026-09-08) records the community tension: *“The Clay problem, as written, is solved. But the Clay problem, as many experts imagine it, lacks the piece that the forcing method relies on… Some in the community may say that the problem hasn’t really been solved or that this has only been achieved through a loophole.”*

### 1.3 Kitchen-table picture

Forced = stirring the coffee with a spoon forever in a carefully designed pattern until the math says a swirl hits infinite speed. Unforced = coffee left alone. Most people asking “can fluids break?” mean the second picture. That is the **correct** problem this filing names.
