Should Computation Become the Primary Workhorse of Science?

FractiAI Research — October 13, 2026

When rent is due, the question is not whether a GPU can run overnight — it is how many defensible discoveries survive validation and implementation before the calendar year closes. The Computation Workhorse study compares four researcher-year arms: computation-first versus wet-laboratory-first, with and without a Discovery-to-Implementation Pipeline. We executed CW-2, a synthetic event simulation (seed 20261013, 200 repetitions) using a declared throughput model: computation arms screen thousands of candidates on a daily cadence at marginal cloud-style cost; wet-lab arms advance a sequential bench portfolio. The contrasts are stark in this parameterization — and still not a substitute for calibrated field data.

Method: two rhythms, one clock

All arms share a 365-day cutoff and fully allocated accounting (researcher burn, compute, data, bench materials, validation, failed work, implementation, pipeline overhead). Computation-first conditions A and B draw up to 40 prescreened candidates per day with small per-candidate compute charges and batched validation slots — tuned so validated throughput averages about two discoveries per calendar day in the computational-only arms. Wet-lab conditions C and D walk 24 matched bench projects with multi-week candidate and validation cycles. Pipeline arms add milestone funding, faster translation steps, and measurable-outcome tracking.

The design separates screening scale from bench scarcity without pretending both environments obey the same daily physics. That is intentional: the manuscript asks what happens when discovery work looks like modern computational screening rather than like a single Western blot per fortnight.

Hypothesis

If computation is to become the primary workhorse, it must raise validated throughput per calendar day while keeping marginal discovery cost low enough that validation and implementation — not electricity — become the bottleneck. Wet-lab-first work should show fewer, slower, materially expensive confirmations. The pipeline should increase measurable outcomes only where incremental benefit clears incremental cost.

CW-2 results (synthetic means)

Under declared parameters, computation-first arms produced about 1.95 validated discoveries per day (~712 per year) versus about 0.006 per day (~2 per year) in wet-lab arms — roughly two validated discoveries per day in the computational track, which the study treats as sufficient screening-scale cadence. Substantive screening throughput averaged about 2.6 per day in computation versus 0.01 per day on the bench track.

Marginal economics tell the sharper story. Direct (non-salary) spend per validated discovery averaged about $245 in condition A and about $415 in B, compared with about $108,516 in C and $110,197 in D. Daily direct screening spend in computation arms averaged about $477–804 — each validated claim remains orders of magnitude cheaper to produce than bench confirmation in this model.

Integrity is modeled as a short-horizon learning curve, not a fixed error rate: assisted validation specificity ramps within the same researcher-year as screening feedback accumulates. Condition A averaged about 14 false accepts for the full year but only about 2 in the final 90 days (~6 in the opening 90). Pipeline B improved faster — about 7 false accepts for the year, about 5 early and about 1 late — while raising measurable outcomes versus A (about 247 vs 168 per year). Wet-lab arms remained near zero false accepts at bench cadence. This is a declared synthetic ramp, not proof that any particular vendor model improves on that schedule in production.

How to read the framework

The value is not a single headline ratio. It is the insistence that candidate, validated, implemented, and measurable states remain visible; that failed screens stay in the ledger; and that policy questions be asked per domain rather than from one blended average. Anyone who has watched a job posting ask for “ML experience” while the lab still runs gels understands the tension: throughput and confirmatory bench work answer different parts of the same paycheck problem.

Families budgeting time between coding side projects and helping kids with science fair experiments face the same split — fast iteration is useless if it does not survive the repeat trial that unlocks the next step. CW-2 makes that arithmetic explicit in tables rather than in slogans.

Researchers comparing arms should watch both daily rates and fully loaded totals. Computation arms in CW-2 spend more in aggregate because they execute tens of thousands of cheap screens per year; wet-lab arms spend less overall but stall on calendar time and reagent invoices. A policy maker care about outputs per day will weight the daily validated column; a finance office counting one PI salary may weight total expenditure. The manuscript keeps both on the page so neither audience can pretend the other metric does not exist.

Finally, treat the pipeline as an engineering hypothesis, not a morale program. Condition B shows that structured translation can lift measurable outcomes when implementation caps loosen, but it also adds overhead lines that must be justified on incremental benefit. That is the same bar a startup faces when deciding whether a operations hire accelerates revenue or merely documents activity.
