Should Computation Become the Primary Workhorse of Science?

FractiAI Research — October 13, 2026

A lab invoice for one GPU hour is easy to compare with a reagent quote. Paychecks, rent, and grant deadlines are not. One competent researcher-year bundles salary, failed experiments, validation queues, partner delays, and the slow work of turning a defensible finding into something that actually changes practice. This Homeostasis Expedition manuscript asks whether computation-first work can deliver more substantive, independently validated discoveries and more implemented progress per dollar and per calendar day than wet-laboratory-first work for the same human — and whether a dedicated Discovery-to-Implementation Pipeline earns its overhead. The title is appropriate; the quantitative answer is not yet measured. What exists today is a prespecified four-condition design, full-cost accounting rules, and reporting templates marked To be calculated — not a completed superiority verdict.

What the design actually compares

The experiment is a two-by-two factorial over one researcher-year and a hard 365-day calendar cutoff. Condition A is human-led computation-first research without a pipeline. B adds the pipeline. C is wet-lab-first without the pipeline. D combines wet-lab-first with the pipeline. Each arm gets matched project portfolios, the same prespecified definitions of substantive discovery and independent validation, and explicit recording of unfinished work at day 365.

That structure separates two effects people often conflate: the discovery environment (compute vs bench) and the translation system (ordinary progression vs milestone funding, partner matching, deployment tracking). A mode that generates ten thousand model candidates is not automatically productive if validation capacity, integrity checks, or implementation funding cap how many claims survive contact with reality.

Research economics: the unit that matters

The economic model insists on fully allocated cost: researcher compensation and benefits, compute and data, facilities and equipment, materials, validation, failed work, implementation, pipeline operations, and overhead — without double-counting. Metrics include cost per substantive discovery, per validated discovery, and per measurable outcome, alongside raw counts because a cheap mode that produces almost nothing is not a bargain.

Comparing a chat API bill to a single Western blot was never the question. The question is how much reliable progress one human can deliver when the entire pathway — including dead ends — is visible in the ledger.

Scientific integrity as an outcome, not a slogan

The manuscript treats funding rules, conflicts of interest, commercial return estimates, and validation sensitivity as variables to be measured in the simulation and, where possible, calibrated against real award and expenditure records. Synthetic incentive rules can explore scenarios; they cannot, by themselves, prove how often institutions actually starve disruptive findings. False accepts, false rejects, and projects stopped for documented reasons must remain in the public record so productivity comparisons are not cleaned to look successful.

That discipline matters to anyone who has watched a promising result die in committee not because it was wrong, but because no one owned the next milestone.

Discovery-to-implementation pipeline

The pipeline is described as continuous operation: capture candidates with provenance, prioritize under explicit criteria, fund milestones, route validation, match partners, deploy, measure endpoints, feed outcomes back into prioritization. Its success criterion is incremental benefit versus incremental cost — not throughput for its own sake.

Appendix tables B1–B3 list the rows investigators must populate after execution: validated discoveries within the year, implementations, measurable outcomes, median times, costs, and projects still waiting on validation or funding at day 365. Every cell currently reads To be calculated by design.

What a decisive result would look like

A completed run would publish, for each condition, total researcher-hours, fully allocated spend, counts of substantive and validated discoveries, implementations, measurable benefits, false accepts where ascertainable, and an end-of-year ledger of unfinished projects with reasons — funding denied, validation backlog, partner delay, or scientific stop. Factorial analysis would estimate main effects of environment and pipeline plus their interaction, with uncertainty carried through cost ratios rather than quoted as single heroic percentages.

Decision criteria are deliberately conservative. Computation-first is favored only when it improves quality-adjusted outcomes or lowers full cost for an equivalent endpoint without unacceptable reliability or safety losses. The pipeline is favored only when incremental outcomes justify incremental cost and risk. A domain where wet-lab evidence remains indispensable should show that in the tables rather than being averaged away.

For parents budgeting time between coding side projects and helping kids with science fair experiments, the metaphor is familiar: the cheap part is not always the fast part, and the fast part is worthless if it does not survive the teacher’s repeat trial. Science policy faces the same arithmetic at grant scale.
