Research note · Recursive homeostasis

When Change, Proportion, and Boundaries Share One Loop, Does Evolution Stay Contained?

FractiAI Research — September 28, 2026

A paycheck that never updates when rent rises is not stability; it is stagnation with a polite face. A budget that rewrites every line every week until the rent number itself has wandered away is not adaptation; it is loss of identity. Between those failures sits a harder question for recursive systems: can a process keep changing while still recognizing itself? The earlier EGS Recursive Fidelity Test asked whether one nest constant — including the golden ratio φ ≈ 1.618 — could reduce cumulative drift on its own. The live answer was clear enough to publish: nothing to crown. Dynamic switching among constants also failed to beat a simple fixed policy. This note is the next expedition, not a rewrite of that baseline. It asks whether a single unified architecture that combines continuous change, proportional structure, and discrete containment can produce useful evolution that stays bounded.

What the live run found

Observed answer — nothing to pin. On the locked fixtures the unified e → φ → prime stack is not a significant improvement over ordinary recursion. Control A (plain neighbor smoothing, no e/φ/prime grammar) spent about 82% of generations inside the Goldilocks band. The combined architecture managed only about 23%. The randomized matched-DOF look-alike (Control B) landed near 50% — still well above the unified arm. A pre-registered significance gate required the combination to beat both controls by a named margin while keeping bleed below Control A and useful evolution positive. Bleed and evolution cleared their checks; the Goldilocks margins did not. Gate: fail. Infinite Octaves engine pin: withheld. Suite integrity (9/9 harness locks) is not hypothesis confirmation.

If you insist on naming who edged the board: ordinary recursion won the primary comparison. Mean final drift favored Control A (~0.54) over unified (~1.39). Unified did post slightly lower mean bleed than Control A and kept useful evolution positive, and late-path drift sat near a nonzero bounded attractor (~1.38) — but that is not enough when the system spends most generations outside the agreed Goldilocks envelope. Ablations make the cost clearer: dropping φ (keeping e and primes) raised the Goldilocks rate to ~81%, nearly matching Control A, while dropping prime containment collapsed the rate to zero under the elevated-bleed proxy. So the live answer to “is it the combination?” is no on these fixtures — composition did not outperform the simple control, and φ inside this stack looked more like a drag than a crown.

The problem after “nothing to crown”

Recursive pipelines — summarizers that rewrite their own summaries, policies that revise policies, household tools that “helpfully” reorganize last month’s categories — fail in two everyday ways. They freeze: the rent line never moves, the job description never absorbs a new duty, the model never admits the world changed. Or they dissolve: each rewrite is locally plausible, yet after twenty passes the document no longer matches what legal approved, what payroll can pay, or what a parent can explain to a child. Engineers often measure one-step error and miss the slope. ERFT measured cumulative drift under matched recursion and refused a magic-number story. That refusal does not end the design problem. It relocates it. If one constant is not enough, and if switching constants is not enough, is there a structured loop in which different mathematical roles share one generation?

Everyday life already suggests a three-part intuition without proving it. Continuous change looks a little like compound growth — the mathematics associated with e. Recognizable proportion under nesting looks a little like self-similar split — the mathematics associated with φ. Containment looks a little like a sealed jar whose size does not factor into smaller integer lids — the mathematics associated with primes. Those are metaphors until an experiment forces them to compete with ordinary smoothing and with a randomized look-alike that has the same number of knobs.

The unified experiment

The architecture under test is deliberately one system. Each generation takes the current container, applies an e-like continuous transform, then a φ-like proportional structuring, then a prime-defined containment step, and emits the next container. The question is not whether drift can be driven to zero. Zero can be stagnation. The question is whether the combined loop can sit in a Goldilocks band: drift above a low floor, below a collapse ceiling, with bleed low and useful evolution positive. In plain language: the system changes, the change stays mostly inside its intended boundaries, and the change still carries recoverable structure rather than pure noise.

Control A is ordinary recursion — neighbor smoothing and mild unstructured drift with no e, φ, or prime grammar. Control B keeps a three-stage shape but replaces the mathematical relationships with random rates, random ratios, and composite (non-prime) sizes, so a win for the unified arm cannot be excused as “more stages always help.” Secondary ablations drop one component at a time to see whether the combination is doing real work. Separate probes compare φ×e formulations and prime scheduling modes — container size, boundary, interval trigger, index — without declaring any formulation the finished EGS architecture in advance.

Hypothesis and falsifiers

The central hypothesis is that recursive homeostasis can emerge as e-dynamics plus φ-structure plus prime containment, producing evolution without dissolution. The experiment must be able to fail. Unified may match or lose to ordinary recursion. Drift may fall while useful evolution also falls — prettier numbers that mean less life in the signal. Prime boundaries may not reduce bleed. φ may add no measurable structural advantage. e-based transformation may destabilize. The three components may interfere. Any apparent edge may vanish under the randomized matched control or collapse into a numerical artifact. Those outcomes are first-class results, not temporary embarrassments waiting for a rescue narrative.

Why the framing still helps even when results are mixed

Methodologically, this expedition keeps three scientific questions from being smuggled into each other. ERFT asked whether a numerical constant alone moves cumulative drift. A grammar lane asked whether nest algebra helps when every arm receives the same operator. A dynamic lane asked whether switching among constants beats the best fixed policy. This paper asks a fourth question beside those three: whether a compositional architecture with distinct candidate roles can produce a bounded evolutionary regime. Separating the questions is the point. It prevents a favorite constant from being smuggled back in as “the combination secretly meant φ all along,” and it prevents a pretty story about primes from being mistaken for zero-bleed physics.

That posture matters outside the lab. People who ship recursive tools for jobs, rent, and kids need a vocabulary that can say “we changed on purpose” without saying “we leaked identity.” Distinguishing drift from bleed is that vocabulary. Distinguishing useful evolution from raw distance is how you refuse to congratulate a system for wandering farther from payroll truth.

What comes next without rewriting the baseline

ERFT-V5 and ERFT-D stay locked. This expedition does not reopen them as “φ won after all,” and the live null here does not become a quiet rescue narrative either. Ordinary recursion beat the combination on the primary Goldilocks scoreboard; engine pin stays off. The honest next step is confirmatory work on a new held-out fixture family with the same unified protocol frozen before looking — or a clear stop if the combination repeatedly fails matched controls. Secondary leads worth one careful follow-up, not a crown: prime indexing did reduce mean bleed versus Control A, and ablating φ nearly recovered ordinary Goldilocks rates, which suggests this particular proportional stage may be the costly piece on these fixtures. Either way, the invitation is practical: read the whitepaper, inspect the primary table against Control A and Control B, and rerun the pipeline from the standalone repository. Change the operators, hold the evaluator still, and see whether your domain shows evolution without dissolution — or another clean, publishable no.
