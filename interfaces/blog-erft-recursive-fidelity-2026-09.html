We Ran the Drift Test. The Favorite Number Lost.

SS Vibelandia — September 25, 2026 · Reno / Truckee corridor

Every executive who has watched an AI stack rewrite last quarter’s playbook knows the quiet fear: the tools still look busy, the dashboards still glow green, and somehow the story that used to match the kitchen table no longer matches the board packet. Call that drift. It is not a lab curiosity. It is how paychecks get mis-aimed, how product promises go soft, and how families lose trust in systems that were supposed to help them keep jobs and rent covered.

This ship just published Version One of a controlled answer. We call it the EGS Recursive Fidelity Test — ERFT for short. The question is simple enough for a dinner table and strict enough for a risk committee: when the same information is transformed again and again by the same recursive process, does baking in the golden nesting ratio near 1.618 reduce how fast meaning drifts, compared with the identical process without that constant, and compared with other famous constants in its place?

We locked the method before we looked at the scoreboard. Then we ran it. The suite executed cleanly. The favorite number did not win. That is not a failure of the product. That is the product working.

Why the boardroom already pays for this problem

Self-rewriting tools are no longer a research demo. They summarize the summary of the summary. They compress the strategy memo into the slide deck, then compress the deck into the agent prompt, then compress the prompt into next week’s “improved” workflow. Each step can look like efficiency. After enough loops, the original customer promise, the original safety line, or the original hours a parent promised a kid after school has wandered out of the room.

People feel that as broken trust long before finance books a line item. A salesperson cannot explain the offer that the agent “optimized.” A school night collapses because the shared calendar helper rewrote its own rules. A freelancer’s billable notes get so polished by automated rewrite that last month’s rent-earning truth is gone. Laid-off workers meet the same pattern on the job board: listings rewritten until the human story no longer matches the opening. Drift is the rent you pay when recursion outruns fidelity.

Companies that ignore that rent ship autonomy theater. Companies that measure it can still automate — they just refuse to confuse motion with progress.

How we designed a fair fight

ERFT is not “our model versus your model.” That contest always hides too many variables. We froze the data, the recursion depth, the evaluator, and the compute habit. Then we changed one dial: the constant threaded into the recursion.

Five arms ran side by side. Baseline used no special constant. One arm used the golden ratio near 1.618. One used the square root of two. One used e, the growth constant every first-year calculus course still teaches. One used random constants drawn from a fixed interval, so nobody could later claim “any number would have helped.” Twenty-four generations. Multiple mechanisms — scaling, recursive weighting, hierarchical resolution, homeostatic feedback, and recursive compression. Proxy series shaped like environmental, astronomical, and synthetic systems with known ground truth.

We also ran a blind constant ladder — unlabeled values including the golden favorite — and a continuous sweep across constants from 1.0 to 2.5 with a train and hold split, so discovering a pretty minimum could not become after-the-fact curve fitting. The win rule was written before the readout: a meaningful win for the golden arm would need lower cumulative drift across domains, survival against decoys, and persistence on held-out series. Absence of that advantage would falsify the strong claim for this Version One fixture set. A null was allowed on purpose.

What Version One actually showed

Here is the part the previous note left out — the results.

All eight locked experiments in the suite passed as protocol. That means the arms ran, the metrics logged, and the comparison stayed fair. It does not mean the golden ratio minimized drift. On the five-arm matched runs, baseline took the lowest final drift on recursive weighting, hierarchical resolution, and recursive compression across the fixture domains we scored. On recursive feedback, e took the lowest final drift in every domain we scored for that mechanism. The golden arm did not dominate a mechanism class.

On the blind constant ladder under recursive compression, the lowest-drift constant was plain baseline at 1.0. The golden ratio near 1.618 ranked fourth. Square-root-of-two and 1.5 sat ahead of it. Larger decoys drifted more. That is the opposite of crowning a favorite.

On the continuous sweep with a held-out split, neither the training minimum nor the hold-out minimum landed near 1.618. Training preferred the high end of the sweep. Hold-out preferred a different neighborhood entirely. If a pronounced valley near the golden ratio had appeared on held-out kinds, that would have been interesting. It did not appear on this fixture set.

Drift-slope summaries under recursive compression showed the first compression bite doing most of the damage, then a flat plateau for the remaining generations — for baseline and for the golden arm alike. The interesting business question was never “who wins generation one by a hair.” It was whether the golden habit changes the shape of error accumulation as recursion continues. On Version One’s locked compression fixture, it did not hand the company a free attractor. The curves stayed honest and unflattering.

What a null result is worth to real operators

Wall Street loves a story where the proprietary constant always wins. Operators who still have to make payroll should prefer a different story: the harness works, the favorite can lose, and the notebook records the loss in public. That is how you keep curiosity doors open for kids who will inherit these tools, and how you keep adult teams from shipping colder doors dressed as destiny.

A null also protects the brand. If we had only published the protocol and left the scoreboard blank, readers would correctly smell marketing. Publishing the numbers — including the ones that do not flatter the catalog’s nesting key — is how a voyage stays credible while it still teaches the nesting habit as a design language worth testing, not a religion worth protecting.

For executives, the management takeaway is sharper than any ratio. Freeze your evaluator. Match your recursion. Change one dial. Plot fidelity against generations. If your preferred nesting habit cannot beat baseline and cannot beat boring decoys on held-out work, do not let the deck claim otherwise. Keep automating where it earns. Keep humans on the fidelity rail where the paycheck, the school night, and the customer promise live.

This is an application companion sitting beside the voyage — a test you can run, not a crown for the golden nesting key. Later versions can attach live public downloads and tighter metrics without rewriting the honesty rule. If a future fixture set shows a persistent golden advantage across domains and decoys, we will publish that too. Until then, the headline stays executive-clear: we ran the drift test, the protocol held, and the favorite number lost fair.

Closing pier

Watch paychecks and product promises, not just model demos. When tools rewrite themselves, drift is the quiet tax on trust. Version One of this recursive fidelity test puts that tax on a scoreboard five arms wide. The suite passed. The golden ratio near 1.618 did not beat baseline on the locked fixtures that mattered most. That honesty is how a ship keeps Main Street in the conversation while the Intelligence Age keeps climbing. Run the recursion. Publish the loss. Keep people able to earn, ask, and belong.
