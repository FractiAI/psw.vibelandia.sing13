When Information Rewrites Itself, Does φ Reduce the Drift?

FractiAI Research — September 26, 2026

Self-improving systems do not fail all at once. They fail by degrees: a summarizer that softens numbers each pass, a policy document that drifts away from what legal approved, a household budget that “helpfully” reorganizes itself until the rent line no longer matches reality. The question behind the EGS Recursive Fidelity Test (ERFT) is deliberately narrow. When the same information is transformed recursively again and again, does explicitly incorporating the golden ratio φ — the EGS fractal constant (1 + √5)/2 ≈ 1.618 — reduce cumulative fidelity drift compared with an otherwise identical process that does not use that nesting geometry?

The problem recursion creates

Recursive and self-improving pipelines treat their own outputs as inputs. That loop is powerful and familiar. It is also where small distortions compound. A model that rewrites a job description each week may preserve the tone while eroding the duties. A classifier that retrains on its own labels can look stable on average while quietly shifting the boundary cases that decide who gets a callback. Engineers often measure one-step error and miss the slope: how fast distance from the original signal grows across generations. ERFT is built around that cumulative curve. It defines Recursive Fidelity Drift (RFD) as the distance between the current transformed series and the original, tracks that distance through depth N, and asks whether the slope changes when only the nesting constant changes.

The experiment does not assume φ is sacred. φ is one arm in a five-arm design, tested beside a dyadic baseline (no special constant), decoy irrationals such as √2 and e, and seeded random constants drawn from a fixed band. Early protocol revisions accidentally gifted easier transforms to the baseline; those runs are kept only as construction receipts. The live protocol (V4) locks transform severity, evaluator, datasets, and recursion depth, then varies only how the constant c partitions each step into nest weights and lag structure. That is the controlled difference: nesting geometry, not “more AI” or “less AI.”

What V4 measured

Each arm runs the same five mechanism classes—scaling, recursive weighting, hierarchical resolution, recursive feedback, and recursive compression—on synthetic ground-truth series and proxy environmental, financial, and biological fixtures. For the golden arm, partition weights use the self-similar split 1/φ and 1/φ² that sum to unity; for the baseline arm, an explicit 1:1 dyadic split. V4 additionally routes every arm through the same catalog-scale fixtures used elsewhere in the Infinite Octaves research stack—clutch slip against the Planck mantissa bridge, a k/81 generation register, octave indexing on a bounded 01–99 map, and odd-prime vault routing—so that φ is tested as a nest key inside a shared grammar, not as a lone magic number pasted onto one transform.

Why test φ at all? Not because the team treats it as a law of nature. The hypothesis is structural: if a process repeatedly nests whole into part and part into remainder, a constant whose whole-to-part ratio equals its part-to-remainder ratio might preserve coherence across iterations better than arbitrary splits. φ is the positive root of that self-similar constraint. The test is whether that algebraic fact translates into lower cumulative drift under matched recursion—a falsifiable claim where a null result is as informative as a win.

On the primary aggregate the protocol pre-registers for matched five-arm runs (experiment E1), mean final RFD across all cells was 9.35 for φ versus 13.87 for the dyadic baseline, roughly thirty-three percent lower drift for the golden nest arm. That gap is large enough to notice in any setting where fidelity is tied to paychecks, compliance, or safety margins, even though the fixtures here are deliberately controlled rather than live production traffic.

The qualification matters as much as the headline. This is not a universal win. On the same E1 scoreboard, scaling still favored the dyadic baseline, recursive weighting and feedback favored seeded decoy constants, and hierarchical resolution split between baseline and decoys. One random decoy posted the lowest mean final RFD overall (8.04), below φ. On the blind constant ladder—where φ is unlabeled among seven candidates—it ranked fourth, behind 1.7, √2, and 1.5 on the illustrative seasonal probe. In the continuous sweep with a train/hold split, minima landed near c = 1.025 on the training slice and at c = 1.0 on hold-out, not near 1.618. Suite pass means the protocol executed cleanly; it does not mean φ minimized every bar.

Why a mixed result still helps

Methodologically, ERFT separates two questions that are often conflated in rhetoric about “aligned” or “self-correcting” AI. First: can you define recursive drift precisely enough to compare arms fairly? Second: under that definition, does a particular geometry move the cumulative curve? V4 answers the first affirmatively for this fixture set and gives a measured, partial answer to the second: φ beats the dyadic baseline on the aggregate E1 mean, yet decoys and simpler constants still win individual mechanism classes and several exploratory probes.

That mixed read is what a senior test engineer should want. It prevents a favorite constant from winning because the control arm inherited softer coarsening, and it prevents a null blind ladder from being hidden because only one mechanism was reported. It also keeps the distinction between a methodological result (the harness locks arms, metrics, and anti-bias rules) and an empirical result (φ lowered mean drift vs baseline here, but did not sweep the field). Neither tier substitutes for CODATA, clinical validation, or a proof about artificial general intelligence safety.

If you build or audit recursive systems, the invitation is practical. Read the full protocol in the whitepaper, inspect the locked arms and section seven readout, and rerun the pipeline yourself. The standalone repository FractiAI/synthobs-egs-recursive-fidelity-test ships the empirical suite under research/synthobs-egs-recursive-fidelity-test/ in the main project; one npm script regenerates the scoreboard from fixtures. Change the nesting constant, hold everything else still, and see whether your domain shows the same aggregate φ advantage, a decoy win, or a null. That is the point of the experiment: not a slogan about golden ratios, but a reproducible place to argue about cumulative drift with numbers on the table.

Honesty rail: ERFT is a controlled catalog companion—a falsifiable drift workbench, not a claim that φ is a physical constant, a universal optimum, or established law. Suite pass means protocol integrity on locked fixtures; the ~33% φ vs baseline mean on E1 is an empirical read that can fail on other domains. Blind ladder rank 4 and sweep minima away from φ bound the strong hypothesis. Prior biased protocol receipts remain footnotes only.
