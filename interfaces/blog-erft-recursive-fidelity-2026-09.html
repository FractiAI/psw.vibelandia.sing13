When Information Rewrites Itself, Does φ Reduce the Drift?

FractiAI Research — September 27, 2026

Self-improving systems do not fail all at once. They fail by degrees: a summarizer that softens numbers each pass, a policy document that drifts away from what legal approved, a household budget that “helpfully” reorganizes itself until the rent line no longer matches reality. The question behind the EGS Recursive Fidelity Test (ERFT) is deliberately narrow. When the same information is transformed recursively again and again, does explicitly incorporating the golden ratio φ — the EGS fractal constant (1 + √5)/2 ≈ 1.618 — reduce cumulative fidelity drift compared with an otherwise identical process that does not use that nesting geometry?

What the live run found

Bottom line: On the locked Version Five fixtures, φ did not win a clean numerical crown. The suite ran cleanly (protocol integrity). The strong claim that φ is the drift-minimizing nest constant for this family is refused. An exploratory dynamic-switching extension also failed to beat the best fixed constant. Earlier Version Four gains stay labeled as development evidence for a combined φ-enabled architecture — not as confirmatory proof that the number alone causes lower drift.

Constant-neutral lane (ERFT-C) — the primary causal test. Named-arm mean final drift (NRMSE, lower is better): baseline 1.387, √2 1.414, φ 1.426, e 1.481. So the headline average slightly favors the plain dyadic baseline over φ. Paired cell differences tell a more nuanced story: φ was lower than baseline in 80% of matched cells, but the mean paired gap was slightly negative for φ (about −0.04), the bootstrap interval crossed zero, and a sign-flip permutation check returned p ≈ 0.77. That is the aggregation trap in plain language — many small wins for φ, a few large losses that pull the mean the other way, and no statistically clean causal win.

Blind ladder and continuous sweep. On the seasonal blind probe, φ ranked fourth of seven constants — mid-pack, not first. The continuous sweep’s train minimum sat near c ≈ 1.025 and the hold minimum elsewhere (about 2.4), both far from 1.618. Depth-dependent optima did not march toward φ as recursion deepened. Those two results falsify the strong optimality claim for these fixtures and are meant to be read as first-class findings, not footnotes.

Grammar lane (ERFT-G) and dynamic extension (ERFT-D). Giving every arm the same generalized nest-closure operator raised mean drift for baseline, φ, √2, and e alike; φ did not benefit disproportionately. Under the separate exploratory dynamic lane, the best fixed constant (mean NRMSE ≈ 0.942) still matched or beat prescribed sequences, state-dependent switching, and adaptive look-ahead. Random switching performed about as well as the state-dependent rule. Removing φ from the adaptive candidate set barely moved the score. The dynamic-regulation hypothesis is therefore weakened on these fixtures — reported as a negative exploratory result, not as a rescue for φ after Version Five.

How to read the scoreboard in one breath. Demonstrated: a fair constant-neutral harness; a mixed/null numerical result for φ versus baseline; optimality refused by the sweep; dynamic switching not yet better than the best fixed constant. Not demonstrated: that φ is the globally best recursive constant, that the number alone causes lower drift, or that multi-constant controllers currently outperform static policies on this board. Suite pass means the protocol executed; it does not mean φ minimized every bar.

The problem recursion creates

Recursive and self-improving pipelines treat their own outputs as inputs. That loop is powerful and familiar. It is also where small distortions compound. A model that rewrites a job description each week may preserve the tone while eroding the duties. A classifier that retrains on its own labels can look stable on average while quietly shifting the boundary cases that decide who gets a callback. Engineers often measure one-step error and miss the slope: how fast distance from the original signal grows across generations. ERFT is built around that cumulative curve. It defines Recursive Fidelity Drift as a scale-normalized distance between the current transformed series and the original, tracks that distance through depth N, and asks whether the slope changes when only the nesting constant changes.

The experiment does not assume φ is sacred. φ is one arm in a multi-arm design, tested beside a dyadic baseline (no special constant), decoy irrationals such as √2 and e, and seeded random constants drawn from a fixed band. Early protocol revisions accidentally gifted easier transforms to the baseline; those runs are kept only as construction receipts. A later revision improved fairness but still gave the golden arm a special recursive affordance — a self-similar re-entry that other constants did not receive — so a lower drift score could mean “φ’s algebra was given a private tool,” not “the number φ alone reduces drift.” Version Five exists to separate those claims.

What Version Five changes

ERFT-V5 runs two layers that must not be conflated. The primary causal lane, ERFT-C, holds the recursive transformation graph invariant and changes only the scalar nest constant c. Every arm gets the same algorithm: unit-normalized nest weights, generation-matched lags and blocks, the same evaluator, the same depth. No constant receives a private closure, a private partition identity, or a private lag budget. That is the experiment that can ask whether the numerical value φ itself matters.

The second lane, ERFT-G, is the architectural hypothesis. It applies a generalized closure operator C(c) that is mathematically available to every constant, then asks whether exploiting nest algebra adds recursive stability — and whether φ benefits disproportionately under the same operator. That is a different scientific question from “does φ reduce drift,” and naming it separately is the point. Prior V4 results remain useful as development evidence for a combined φ-enabled architecture; they are not treated as confirmatory proof that the number alone caused the improvement.

Why test φ at all? Not because the team treats it as a law of nature. The hypothesis is structural: if a process repeatedly nests whole into part and part into remainder, a constant whose whole-to-part ratio equals its part-to-remainder ratio might preserve coherence across iterations better than arbitrary splits. φ is the positive root of that self-similar constraint. The test is whether that algebraic fact translates into lower cumulative drift under matched recursion — a falsifiable claim where a null result is as informative as a positive one.

How the scoreboard stays honest

Each arm runs the same five mechanism classes — scaling, recursive weighting, hierarchical resolution, recursive feedback, and recursive compression — on synthetic ground-truth series and proxy environmental, financial, and biological fixtures. Drift is reported as normalized RMSE so domains with different numeric scales do not dominate the aggregate. For every matched cell the suite also stores the paired difference between baseline and φ, then summarizes mean, median, spread, the fraction of cells where φ is lower, a bootstrap interval, and a simple sign-flip permutation check. That structure matters when one domain could otherwise hide inside a flattering average.

On the live constant-neutral board, that honesty is not decorative. Named-arm mean NRMSE slightly favored the dyadic baseline (about 1.39 versus 1.43 for φ), yet φ still posted lower drift in eighty percent of matched cells — a classic aggregation trap where a minority of large opposite cells can flip the headline mean. The bootstrap interval on the paired difference crossed zero; a sign-flip permutation check did not reject the null. Under ERFT-C, this fixture set does not yet establish a clean numerical causal advantage for φ.

The continuous sweep is treated as a first-class result, not a footnote. Train minima sat near c ≈ 1.025 and hold minima elsewhere — not near 1.618 — so the strong optimality claim is refused for these fixtures. A depth-dependent probe asked whether the minimizing constant marches toward φ as recursion deepens; on the seasonal weighting probe it did not. Symmetric generalized closure in the grammar lane raised mean drift for every named arm rather than rescuing a structural φ advantage. Random constants remain percentile controls rather than crown candidates.

Suite pass means the protocol executed cleanly with the constant-neutral and grammar layers locked. It does not mean φ minimized every bar. An engineering fifteen-percent band is documented as an operational reporting threshold, not a statistically validated effect size. Shape fidelity (correlation) is kept separate from amplitude fidelity so a scaled-up copy of the original cannot look perfect while being wrong in magnitude.

Why a careful mixed result still helps

Methodologically, ERFT separates questions that are often conflated in rhetoric about “aligned” or “self-correcting” AI. First: can you define recursive drift precisely enough to compare arms fairly? Second: under a constant-neutral definition, does a particular number move the cumulative curve? Third: under a symmetric grammar, does exploiting nest algebra add anything further? Version Five answers the first affirmatively for this fixture set and keeps the second and third from being smuggled into each other.

That posture is what a senior experimental architect should want. It prevents a favorite constant from winning because the control arm inherited softer coarsening, and it prevents a strong blind-ladder result from being mistaken for a universal crown. It also keeps the distinction between development evidence from earlier protocol generations and confirmatory evidence under the locked constant-neutral lane. Neither tier substitutes for CODATA, clinical validation, or a proof about artificial general intelligence safety.

What comes next without rewriting the baseline

A fourth question sits beside those three, not on top of them. Maybe recursive stability does not come from one magic number at all. Maybe it comes from switching among several transformation modes as the system’s own drift state changes — preservation when error is rising, reorganization when a path is recovering, and so on. ERFT-D puts that idea under a separate exploratory lane: fixed constants as controls, prescribed sequences, state-dependent switching that may only read present observables, and an adaptive one-step look-ahead that is not allowed to peek at held-out futures. φ remains one candidate among peers. If the best fixed constant still matches or beats the dynamic policies, or if an adaptive controller quietly collapses to a single constant, the hypothesis is weakened and the report says so. The extension leaves Version Five’s mixed constant-neutral readout intact, and treats dynamic switching as a falsifiable follow-on rather than a finished homeostasis proof.

If you build or audit recursive systems, the invitation is practical. Read the full protocol in the whitepaper, inspect the locked arms and section seven readout, and rerun the pipeline yourself. The standalone repository FractiAI/synthobs-egs-recursive-fidelity-test ships the empirical suite under research/synthobs-egs-recursive-fidelity-test/ in the main project; one npm script regenerates the scoreboard from fixtures. Change the nesting constant, hold everything else still, and see whether your domain shows a paired advantage for φ, a decoy edge, a sweep minimum elsewhere, or a null. That is the point of the experiment: not a slogan about golden ratios, but a reproducible place to argue about cumulative drift with numbers on the table.

Honesty rail: ERFT is a controlled catalog companion—a falsifiable drift workbench, not a claim that φ is a physical constant, a universal optimum, or established law.
