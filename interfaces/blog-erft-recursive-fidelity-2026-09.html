Does Geometry Slow the Drift?

FractiAI Research — September 26, 2026 · ERFT-V4-2026-09-26

Any system that rewrites itself accumulates a tax. A language model fine-tuned on its own outputs, a supply-chain optimizer that ingests its own forecasts, a medical-records platform that summarizes summaries — each generation of recursion moves the output further from the original. The question is not whether drift happens. It does. The question is whether the shape of the recursion matters.

Recursive drift is well-documented in machine learning, information theory, and systems engineering, but measuring it cleanly is harder than it looks. Most evaluations conflate the depth of recursion with the severity of each individual transform, or they test a favored method against a control arm that inherits a subtly easier problem by design. The EGS Recursive Fidelity Test was built to avoid both traps — and to ask a more pointed question: if you hold everything else constant and change only the nesting geometry, does the cumulative information loss change?

The setup is deliberately austere. Take a dataset. Apply a recursive transformation — scaling, hierarchical reconstruction, feedback-weighted rewriting, compression. Do this twenty-four times. After each generation, measure how far the output has drifted from the original, using root-mean-square error as the primary score. Repeat across five arms where the only variable is the constant threaded into the nesting geometry. The data, the evaluator, the recursion structure, and the depth are identical across all arms. The nesting ratio is the single controlled difference.

One arm uses a plain dyadic split: each step divides the nest one-to-one, which is what a system does when no particular geometry is assumed. Another arm uses φ — the golden ratio, approximately 1.618 — the value where the ratio of the whole to the larger part equals the ratio of the larger part to the smaller. Three further arms use √2, e, and randomly drawn constants as decoys, because if any unusual constant reduces drift, the finding is less meaningful than if only φ, with its specific self-similar property, does so.

Why φ at all? Not because it is assumed to be correct. The hypothesis is narrower: φ satisfies the equation 1/φ + 1/φ² = 1, which means the whole partitions into parts that maintain the same ratio at every level. If cumulative fidelity loss is partly a function of how partition geometry accumulates across recursive generations, that self-similarity offers a falsifiable prediction. An experiment designed so that a null result would count as a genuine and informative finding.

What the experiment found

Version 4 of the harness grounds the nesting geometry in the Infinite Octaves engine catalog — incorporating octave-band indexing tied to recursion depth, k/81 scale registers, and odd-prime vault routing as the structural framework through which each arm is evaluated. The core result on the matched five-arm runs (the E1 experiment class) across synthetic and proxy domains: the golden arm produced a mean final Recursive Fidelity Drift of approximately 10.7, compared with 13.8 for the dyadic baseline — roughly 23 percent lower cumulative drift in the aggregate that the protocol is designed to measure.

The picture by mechanism class is more granular. φ won two of five categories outright: recursive feedback and recursive compression, the two mechanisms most structurally similar to the kind of self-referential updating that matters in AI systems and organizational processes. Scaling on these fixtures still favored the dyadic baseline. Recursive weighting went to e; hierarchical resolution went to a random decoy. In the blind ladder — where constants are presented without labels and ranked by aggregate performance across all mechanisms — φ placed third out of seven candidates. The continuous sweep across the interval from 1.0 to 2.5, evaluated on held-out series to prevent curve-fitting, found its minima at 1.35 in training and 1.025 on hold-out. Neither is near 1.618.

What those results do not say

This qualification matters as much as the aggregate result. The 23 percent advantage is real within the V4 fixture set, but φ did not win uniformly across every dimension of the test. It placed third on the blind ladder, not first. Scaling preferred simpler dyadic geometry. The sweep found a minimum well away from the golden value on held-out data. These are not complications to bury in a footnote — they are the substance of the result. A framework in which φ always won every bar would be more suspect, not more convincing, because it would suggest the test was designed to confirm what it set out to measure.

What the experiment establishes instead is a protocol structure that makes the question answerable in principle and contestable in practice. The fixture locks prevent construction bias — the mechanism that allowed the first version of this test, V1, to give the control arm a softer problem by design, making baseline appear to win by inheriting the mildest treatment. A train/hold split on the sweep prevents reading a well-fit curve as a general result. The blind ladder prevents selecting which mechanism class to advertise after observing which one φ happened to win. Prior protocol versions are in the paper as footnotes rather than silently overwritten, so anyone comparing numbers across reports can trace the lineage.

The honest characterization of ERFT-V4 is this: under controlled conditions with matched recursion depth and identical evaluators, using the golden ratio as the nesting geometry reduced mean cumulative fidelity drift by approximately 23 percent against the dyadic baseline, with class wins concentrated in recursive feedback and compression, and without a full sweep or blind-ladder crown. That is a methodological result and a partial empirical result within a specific fixture set. Whether the advantage generalizes to production systems at arbitrary scale, or whether recursive geometry is the dominant driver of drift in real-world AI deployments, remains to be studied. Those are open questions. This experiment is a step toward being able to ask them rigorously.

The reason this kind of test is worth running — and worth running honestly — is that drift in recursive systems is a real engineering and organizational problem. When self-updating tools compound their own errors, the cost shows up in places that are difficult to audit: policy documents that no longer match the decisions they were meant to encode, training datasets that gradually reflect model outputs rather than ground truth, recommendation systems that reinforce the patterns they were hired to correct. People who work with these systems — in operations, clinical settings, financial services, or any domain where AI tools update themselves — rarely get visibility into this accumulation. A falsifiable framework for testing whether geometric choices affect that accumulation is a practical instrument, not a theoretical exercise. Knowing that φ does not sweep the field is as useful as knowing it wins in aggregate — both facts constrain the hypothesis space for future, better-powered tests.

The paper holds the full scoreboard: class-by-class winners, ladder rankings, sweep curves by mechanism, and the construction-integrity locks that define what a passing result means. The standalone repository runs the complete pipeline — synthetic series, proxy domains, all five arms, all five mechanism classes — with a single command, and the fixtures are deterministic enough that rerunning with a different seed does not change which arm produced a self-similar partition. If the framing of this result interests you — whether the geometry of recursion shapes information longevity, and whether that relationship can be measured with enough precision to be useful — the paper is the place to engage with it.
