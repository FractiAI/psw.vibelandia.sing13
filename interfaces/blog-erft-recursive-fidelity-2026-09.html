Why Version One confused the pier

Every operator who has watched an AI stack rewrite last quarter’s playbook knows the quiet tax: the tools still look busy, the dashboards still glow green, and somehow the story that used to match the kitchen table no longer matches the board packet. Call that drift. It is how paychecks get mis-aimed, how product promises go soft, and how families lose trust in systems that were supposed to help them keep jobs and rent covered.

Version One of this harness published a humble-looking null: baseline kept posting the lowest drift on the mechanisms that matter most. Player 1 said the quiet part out loud — baseline should not produce the least drift. That is a fair-fight rule, not a brand preference. Opening the notebook showed why. On hierarchical resolution and recursive compression, block size grew with the constant, so a constant of one got the finest mesh — the mildest mash. Scaling and recursive weighting sat near identity. Feedback used gain one-over-c, so larger constants looked calmer by construction. The scoreboard was not lying about the math it ran. It was lying about the question we thought we asked.

That is the executive nightmare in miniature. You freeze the evaluator. You match the recursion. You still let the control arm inherit the softest job. Then you publish a null that sounds humble while the harness never gave the favorite — or the decoys — a fair chance. Version One stays on file as the bug receipt. It is not the live board.

What Version Two changed in the dial

ERFT is still not “our model versus your model.” Data, depth, evaluator, and compute habit stay frozen. The only intentional difference is still the constant threaded into the recursion. Five arms still run side by side: baseline at one, the golden ratio near 1.618, square root of two, e, and seeded random draws from a fixed interval.

What changed is the meaning of that dial. Transform severity is now fixed across arms. The constant picks geometry — which chord to shear along, which pair of fixed lags to blend, which phase to reinject after a shared compression bite — not how hard the machine mashes the series. Hierarchical and compression blocks no longer grow with the constant. Feedback no longer hides behind inverse gain. Scaling and weighting no longer sit at identity. The new lock fails the suite if those cheat codes return: identity traps, monotone coarsening, inverse-gain feedback, or baseline owning a majority of mechanism classes by construction.

That is the same discipline a payroll team already understands. If two contractors bid the same scope, you do not hand the control bidder a shorter punch list and then congratulate yourself when they “win” on cost. You keep the scope matched. You change one variable. You read the invoice.

Why a clear version label matters on Main Street

Wall Street loves a story where the proprietary constant always wins. Operators who still have to make payroll should prefer a different story: when the harness is wrong, you fix the harness in public; when the favorite only partly wins, you say partly; when the held-out sweep points somewhere else, you leave that door open for the next kid who will inherit these tools.

Parents trying to keep a school night coherent, freelancers trying to keep billable notes true enough to cover rent, and teams trying to keep a customer promise from dissolving under agent rewrites all need the same habit: freeze the evaluator, match the recursion, change one dial, and refuse to let the control arm inherit the softest job. Laid-off workers meeting rewritten job boards know the same pattern — listings that wander until the human story no longer matches the opening.

For executives, the management takeaway is sharper than any ratio. If your preferred nesting habit only “wins” because the baseline mash was milder, you do not have a nesting insight — you have a softer punch list. If a deck still quotes Version One’s baseline wins as if they were today’s science, throw the deck back. The live protocol is Version Two.
