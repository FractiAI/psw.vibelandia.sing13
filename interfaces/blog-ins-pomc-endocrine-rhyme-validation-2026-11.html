Insulin and Appetite: Can a Rhyme Pair Earn a Real-Data Check?

FractiAI Research — November 19, 2026

A paycheck does not care whether a pattern came from a telescope archive or a gene panel. It cares whether the pattern points at something that already shapes grocery carts, clinic wait lists, and whether someone can keep a job through a diabetes diagnosis or an obesity diagnosis. After the Pilot Rhyme and LLM Rhyme theaters listed hundreds of named partners from catalogs already on disk, we asked a sharper follow-up: which single pair, if it held under public data and math hygiene, would matter most to people at scale — and can we validate that choice without pretending geometry is a wet lab?

The problem on the ground

Rhymes are good at surfacing held-out neighbors that flat local windows miss. That skill is useful and easy to overclaim. A deep-sky twin is interesting. A microwave multipole twin is interesting. A gene-exon twin that happens to name insulin beside an appetite peptide is a different kind of interesting, because diabetes and obesity already sit inside household budgets and public health ledgers worldwide. The risk is obvious: treat a feature-space rhyme as if it were a finished pathway, then sell hope. The opposite risk is quieter: leave the highest-stakes partner on the catalog shelf and never ask the public databases whether the literature already knows the names.

We therefore did not launch a new telescope expedition. We launched a triage expedition. Among the theater partners, three metabolic and neuro pairs competed. INS with POMC carries insulin and the POMC appetite peptide. APP with DYRK1A carries Alzheimer and neurodevelopment literature themes. NPY with SOD1 couples a neuropeptide to an antioxidant enzyme with high geometric score and no STRING edge. The pick rule was explicit: estimated population disease burden, times external STRING association, times rhyme recovery. INS↔POMC won. That is a reason to look carefully, not a license to prescribe.

What we tested

The lab is regenerable Node code on public fixtures. Exon features come from the same GTEx and MyGene panels used upstream. We recomputed z-scored vectors and cosine partners for INS and POMC exons, then ranked every cross-gene pair in the ten-gene panel. We ran a feature-shuffle permutation null so a high score could not hide behind a silent baseline. Separately, we cached STRING network edges for INS–POMC, APP–DYRK1A, and NPY–SOD1, and cached OpenTargets disease rows for insulin’s diabetes rails and POMC’s obesity rails. Finally, we proved two operator lemmas in code: cosine is symmetric, and each z-scored feature column has mean near zero. Those lemmas are math hygiene for the lab, not biology theorems.

Hypothesis

If the rhyme-recovered INS↔POMC partnership is more than a catalog curiosity, then three things should line up without forcing a win. The computational lane should at least reproduce the upstream exon hit. The external association lane should show a literature edge stronger than the runners-up, with a geometric-only pair failing as a negative control. The disease rails should sit on diabetes and obesity rather than on unrelated labels. If geometry alone crowned the pair inside the panel null, that would be a stronger uniqueness claim. We did not require that crown to justify follow-up triage; we required honesty about whether it appeared.

Results

The computational lane reproduced the hit. The best INS↔POMC exon pair was INS exon 2 on chromosome 11 with POMC exon 2 on chromosome 2, coupling about 0.74 — the same partnership the theater catalogs already named. Inside the ten-gene panel, though, that pair ranked thirteenth of forty-five cross-gene couples. A feature-shuffle permutation returned a p-value near 0.35. Geometry alone does not make INS↔POMC the unique twin in this panel. Several other couples score higher on features, including NPY↔SOD1.

The external lane is where the pick earns its keep. STRING returns a 0.947 association for INS with POMC, driven almost entirely by text-mining evidence, with experimental evidence at zero on the cached edge. APP with DYRK1A sits at 0.57. NPY with SOD1 returns an empty network — the negative control that high geometry without literature should look like. OpenTargets puts insulin on diabetes rails (type 1 and type 2 among them) and POMC on obesity rails, including POMC-deficiency obesity. The mankind-benefit gate score is about 0.70 for INS↔POMC against about 0.27 for APP↔DYRK1A and zero for NPY↔SOD1. Math lemmas all passed.

In plain language: the rhyme found a pair that public literature already co-mentions on a metabolic axis that pays rent in human suffering. The same rhyme can also find pairs the literature does not support. That is why the expedition exists — to separate triage-worthy follow-ups from geometric lookalikes before anyone confuses a cosine with a clinic.

Qualifications

This is an application companion, not an engine shelf pin. It is not a causal pathway proof, not a clinical effect, not medical advice, and not a wet-lab result. STRING’s high score here is literature association, not experimental proof of a physical binding that the rhyme invented. OpenTargets disease scores are platform associations, not prescriptions. The panel is a public demonstration set of ten genes; ranks inside it are not genome-wide claims. The honest reading is mixed on uniqueness and supportive on follow-up triage.

Why the framework still earns the work

Rhymes earn their keep when they turn catalogs you already paid for into named observations you can take to the next table — literature databases, disease rails, and math hygiene — without demanding a new instrument first. Pilot Rhyme and LLM Rhyme showed that Director maps and awareness boards can recover held-out partners flat windows miss. This expedition shows what to do next with the highest-stakes partner on that list: test it in public, keep the negative control, and refuse to upgrade a triage label into a miracle. For teams hiring agents to explore real archives, that loop — rhyme, name, validate, qualify — is the product habit worth copying.
