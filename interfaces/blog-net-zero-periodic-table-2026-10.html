Do Group 13 and Group 15 Electronegativity Gaps Shrink Down the Table?

FractiAI Research — October 31, 2026

A baking recipe that lists sugar but forgets salt still looks like a recipe until the cookies taste wrong. A payroll spreadsheet that tracks wages but not withholdings invents a surprise on payday. Chemistry’s periodic table is the opposite temptation: it looks so complete that people stop asking which columns are complementary accounts of the same shell story. Net Zero Expedition 002 takes the Missing Half model from Expedition 001 and drives it onto one sharp question: when you pair Group 13 with Group 15 period by period, do their electronegativity differences show a consistent period-dependent pattern—and does that pattern survive when you refuse to trust a single textbook scale?

Why these two columns

Group 13 elements—boron through thallium—carry three valence electrons. Group 15 elements—nitrogen through bismuth—carry five. Add them and you recover eight, the familiar shell target that students meet before they ever hear the word electronegativity. Under Expedition 001’s grammar, that is not a cute coincidence; it is an accounting identity. The groups sit on either side of carbon’s column like two halves of a ledger that both have to clear if the period’s story is about completing a shell.

Electronegativity is how chemists talk about how hard an atom pulls shared electrons in a bond. Parents sorting a shared custody calendar already know the intuition: some partners pull harder on time, attention, or groceries. The question here is narrower. For each period, subtract the Group 13 electronegativity from the Group 15 value. Call that gap Δχ. Does Δχ stay positive? Does it tend to shrink as you walk down the table? And if the answer looks tidy on Pauling’s famous scale, does it still look tidy on Allen’s spectroscopic scale, on Allred–Rochow’s electrostatic scale, and when you switch the property entirely to ionization energy or covalent radius?

What the lab actually measured

The suite embeds tabulated literature values—not a new wet-lab campaign—so anyone can rerun the arithmetic offline. On Pauling numbers, the five period gaps are about 1.00 (B/N), 0.58 (Al/P), 0.37 (Ga/As), 0.27 (In/Sb), and 0.40 (Tl/Bi). Every gap is positive: Group 15 pulls harder than its Group 13 partner in the same period. The association with period number is negative (Spearman ρ ≈ −0.70). That is a consistent period-dependent attenuation without demanding a perfectly straight staircase; the small rebound at period 6 is kept visible rather than smoothed away, because heavy-element quirks are part of the honesty, not a bug to hide.

Independent scales were not decorative. Allen and Allred–Rochow each kept all gaps positive, kept a negative association with period, and stayed rank-concordant with the Pauling gap series (Spearman ρ ≈ 0.70 on this five-point board). First ionization energy differences—the energy to remove an electron—tracked the electronegativity gaps with the same rank concordance and also declined with period. Covalent radii supplied a geometric complement: for periods two through five, the Group 15 atom is smaller than its Group 13 partner, and the size gap tracks the electronegativity story; period six is the soft edge where thallium and bismuth nearly meet.

A permutation check shuffles the Allen gaps against the Pauling series thousands of times. The observed concordance is uncommon under that shuffle (approximate p ≈ 0.11 on seed 20261031). With only five periods the null is coarse, so the paper treats it as supportive hygiene, not a courtroom verdict.

Hypothesis under the Missing Half model

If complementary structure is more than column wallpaper, then (1) Group 13↔15 should admit a clean involution-plus-octet account, (2) Δχ should show a period-dependent pattern under declared metrics, (3) that pattern should survive independent electronegativity definitions, (4) at least one independent non-electronegativity property should concord, and (5) Expedition 001’s non-uniqueness lesson should appear as multi-scale completion rather than a single sacred table. The engine-shelf seat stays empty: this is an application companion on the Net Zero journey, and it deliberately does not try to re-derive the periodic table from the golden-ratio design grammar used elsewhere in the catalog.

What counts as novel here

Expedition 002 also ships an explicit catalog—NZ-PT-01 through NZ-PT-08—of suite-framed observations produced by applying the Missing Half model. “Novel” means newly framed and jointly scored in this ledger: the involution-plus-octet account; the period-dependent Pauling pattern; Allen and Allred–Rochow survival; ionization-energy concordance; radius complement with a named period-six soft edge; the multi-scale non-uniqueness lesson; and the distributed cross-property ledger itself. Chemists long ago noticed that nitrogen pulls harder than boron; the point of the ledger is different. The Net Zero vehicle makes the period-paired relationships testable as a complementary account with independent-source survival gates and a machine-readable observation list.

Under seed 20261031, experiments E1 through E8 passed eight of eight, and the novel observation catalog reported eight of eight supported. Rent is still due in the usual sense: tabulated numbers can be wrong, five periods are a short series, and none of this is medical or toxicology advice. The Digit 4 beryllium–oxygen companion remains a separate filing; this expedition leaves that seat alone.
