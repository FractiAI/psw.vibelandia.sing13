Upgrade the World Model, Not the Org Chart

FractiAI Research — November 15, 2026

When a clinic, a school office, and a landlord each keep their own copy of the same family's paperwork, nobody is short of data. They are short of a shared picture, and the family pays for it in repeated forms, missed deadlines, and rent that comes due before the one clause connecting three folders gets found. Multi-agent systems have the same problem at machine speed. The more capable the agents, the less their limit is intelligence and the more it is the world model they are asked to coordinate through. We asked whether changing that model, rather than the agents or the org chart, would measurably help.

The problem with chains of private copies

Most multi-agent designs still follow one pattern. An agent gets a role, a rule, and a plan, then passes messages to the next agent toward a fixed objective. We call this the Legacy Linear world model. It is predictable, and it holds up well when tasks are stable and well specified. It strains when capable agents could discover relationships nobody planned for, because every agent keeps a private copy of the context and every extra agent adds another copy to keep in agreement. Teams see it as slower coordination, fewer agents per machine, and more reconciliation before anyone trusts a combined result. People see the same pattern at work when a job title stands in for a whole person and the person who could have solved the problem sits outside the template.

What we tested and what we measured

The alternative we call the Fractal Holographic Goldilocks world model. Agents work inside one shared world. Each holds only its own differences from that world and reconstructs the rest on demand. Boundaries and consent decide who sees what. The aim is a band with enough structure to stay coherent and safe, and enough freedom for agents to use the capability they have.

FractiSI Multi-Agent is the computational lab. The suite runs six experiments from a fixed seed, using public data and our own earlier receipts, and every number below regenerates from the repository. Expedition 002, which we cite rather than rerun, showed guided reconstruction solving all 100 six-hop discovery tasks where flat skimming solved none, in three coordination rounds instead of six and six operations instead of forty.

On public Federal Reserve series, a walk-forward forecast of next-month unemployment over 805 windows scored an RMSE of 5.675 when it respected time order and 6.115 when we scrambled the sequence, so order carries real signal. A simpler bag-of-features baseline scored 5.673, essentially tied with the temporal model, and we report that plainly: on this series the temporal model is not uniquely better. On Movebank moose tracks joined to the archived geomagnetic Kp index, lag-one structure in daily movement was about 0.34 against about 0.04 for a shuffled null.

The coordination results are about efficiency at parity. A shared-world arm cut duplicated bytes by about 91 percent against a competition-first arm with identical direction accuracy, 0.30 in both. Selective open sharing cut bytes by about 87 percent against closed copies at matched error. These are not accuracy gains, and nothing here shows a model reasons better. They show that the same answers cost far less to coordinate when agents share a world.

We also scored four world models on capability, safety, cost, sustainability, and participation quality, using dimensions the suite measured. Goldilocks alignment scored 76.5, ahead of unbounded openness at 75, competition-first at 71, and control-first at 66.5. The lead over unbounded openness is small and the weights are ours, so read it as a lab scoreboard, not a verdict.

Where this goes for organizations

The same question applies to any organization building on accelerated computing, from chips through models to agent fleets and the teams around them. The first move is an audit, not a reorganization: how agents are defined, how the world is represented, where context is duplicated, how dense the agent population is, where boundaries sit, and where flow stalls. A contractor keeping ten private copies of the same binder is a fair picture of what the audit looks for. The goal is to locate the mismatch between the world model in use and the capability already present.

The approach should be rejected or revised if shared worlds show no advantage under matched budgets, if added autonomy hurts results, if consent-based coordination loses to central control, or if migration adds complexity without a safer, cheaper, or more usable system. The next decisive experiment is the one we have not run: the same models, prompts, tools, and budgets, with only the information architecture changed.
