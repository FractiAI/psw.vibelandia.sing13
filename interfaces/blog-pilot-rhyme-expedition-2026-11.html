Pilot Rhyme: One Map-Holder, Many Local Agents, One Shared World

FractiAI Research — November 18, 2026

Anyone who has watched a project team burn a paycheck on meetings knows the pattern. One person is supposed to hold the map. Everyone else is supposed to run a local step. Instead, each person keeps a private copy of the whole binder, the wording of the next ticket changes every hour, and the merge at the end of the day is where the overtime lives. Agent fleets are starting to look the same. This expedition asks whether a reusable Director and Pilot role pattern — stored once, with each mission kept as only what differs, inside one shared world — does measurable work that flat peers and exact-text caches do not.

The problem on the job

Coding agents, research swarms, and enterprise copilots are shifting from one assistant to many agents on one project. Memory bandwidth and merge friction are already the bottleneck. The expensive habit is reprinting the same role sheet and the same map into every private context, then hoping an exact-text cache will notice when tomorrow’s ticket uses different words for the same job. On a job board of missions that share a structure but not a sentence, that cache stays quiet. The team still pays for the copies.

Earlier FractiSI expeditions already showed that a rhyming shared world helps when the structure repeats and the words change. Protein Folding Rhyme and LLM Rhyme added ways to recover long-range partners from relationship maps. What was still missing was a named feature for the everyday coordination split itself: one seat that holds the map, and other seats that run local steps, reused across many missions without reprinting the roles.

What we tested

We built a regenerable lab of one hundred six-step missions. Every mission used the same Director and Pilot role structure. Every mission used different wording for the local steps. Three Pilots worked under one Director. We compared five arms on the same seed.

Flat peers skimmed private slices with no roles. A shared text board dumped briefs into one blob without role structure. Director and Pilot with private copies gave every seat a full map. Director and Pilot with an exact-text cache stored role-plus-mission packets only when the bytes were new. Pilot Rhyme stored the role template once, wrote each mission as deltas into a shared world, and let the Director hold map edges while Pilots read local hops.

Hypothesis

If the hard part of multi-agent work is repeating structure under changing words, then Pilot Rhyme should recover every mission that flat peers miss, and should store the work in less space than an exact-text role cache that never hits when the wording differs. Private Director and Pilot copies might match the accuracy — roles alone can walk a chain — but they should not match the size.

Results

Flat peers recovered zero of one hundred missions. The shared text board also recovered zero; dumping briefs into one place without a role structure did not walk the chain. All three Director and Pilot arms recovered one hundred of one hundred. The exact-text role cache recorded zero hits across the hundred differently worded missions. Pilot Rhyme’s store was twenty-two percent smaller than that exact-text cache and eighty percent smaller than private Director and Pilot copies.

In plain language: roles are what find the answer. Rhyme is what keeps the binder from growing every time the ticket text changes. That is the same shape we already saw when rhyming templates beat exact caches on protein notes and moose GPS tracks.

Qualifications

Private Director and Pilot copies also scored one hundred of one hundred, so the accuracy gain against flat peers is the role structure, not a magic that only rhyme owns. The differentiated claim for Pilot Rhyme is store size under differing words, plus the product lock that those seats write into one shared world instead of private reprints. The lab is architectural and structured-state. It is not CapEx, not a field robot trial, and not a language-model bake-off. The Director and Pilot idea is taken from a published coordination pattern; the seats are rebuilt in-house. We do not ship or depend on non-commercial model weights.

Candidates on real public theaters

The same Director and Pilot seats also run on the public sky and genome panels used elsewhere in the rhyme work. Flat peers that only see nearby rows recover none of the long-range held-out partners. With the Director holding the relationship map and Pilots holding local slices in one shared world, Pilot Rhyme recovers every long-range task in the lab and lists hundreds of named pairs — exoplanet couples such as Kepler-607 c with TOI-1758 b, close-approach encounter pairs, deep-sky object pairs, microwave multipole pairs, and demo exon pairs such as INS with POMC. Those rows are candidates for follow-up, not finished discoveries, and the genome panel stays a demonstration.

Why this earns a feature seat

FractiSI Multi-Agent’s World Model now carries five rhyme features. Rhyming Templates reuse structure across sessions. Shared-World Rhyme lets agents that each see a slice write into one world. Protein Folding Rhyme turns a sequence into a relationship board. LLM Rhyme writes that board as a language pack a model can use. Pilot Rhyme is the coordination seat: Director holds the map, Pilots run local steps, roles live once, missions arrive as deltas.

For teams hiring agents the way they hire people — one lead with the plan, several hands on local tickets — that is the pattern that shows up on the floor. The lab says the pattern finds the chain when flat skimming does not, and that keeping it as a rhyme costs less space than pretending every new wording is a brand-new packet.
