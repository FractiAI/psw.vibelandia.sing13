When Tools Rewrite Themselves, Does φ Keep Meaning Intact?

FractiAI Research — September 22, 2026

When software begins modifying its own code, prompts, or operational parameters, ordinary error-handling assumptions break down. The output of iteration N becomes the operating environment of iteration N+1. A hiring assistant that softens its own criteria each week, a household budget tool that “helpfully” reorganizes categories until the rent line no longer matches reality, or a policy summarizer that retrains on its own paraphrases can look busy while quietly leaving the original job. The question behind this note is narrow and falsifiable. Do recursive self-improvement loops create unique fidelity requirements—and does locking a nesting invariant at the golden ratio φ ≈ 1.618 preserve semantic coherence across iterations better than unstructured loops or simple rational scaling?

The problem self-rewrite creates

Linear, one-pass software can tolerate significant local errors through retry and rollback. When systems begin modifying themselves, errors compound instead of staying isolated. A parent process that edits its own prompts from previous outputs creates a feedback loop where mistakes become training data for the next pass. That pattern showed up across domains in this work: assistants that retune their own behavioral parameters and drift into inconsistent replies; automated inventory systems that optimize themselves into contradictory policies; content tools that gradually leave their original specifications as they learn from their own outputs.

Simple iteration repeats the same process with different inputs. Recursive self-improvement means the process modifies itself from its outputs, then re-enters at a deeper scale using those modifications as context. Kids notice a softer version when rules at home change every night without a principle that survives the rewrite; adults notice it when job boards, compliance checklists, and safety margins depend on tools that no longer mean what they meant last month. The stability requirement is qualitatively different from one-shot automation.

What the fidelity harness measured

Multiple self-modifying systems ran in controlled environments while the team tracked how well original objectives held across recursive iterations. Key metrics included semantic consistency, policy coherence, and alignment with initial specifications over extended operation. Systems fell into three groups: no structural invariants; simple rational scaling (powers of two, simple fractions); and φ-based nesting ratios. Each group ran identical tasks over equivalent timeframes, with regular checkpoints on output quality and objective alignment.

The hypothesis centered on whether a specific mathematical ratio could supply structural stability. φ is the positive solution of the self-similar equation x = 1 + 1/x, and its continued fraction [1; 1, 1, 1…] is maximally resistant to rational approximation. The testable claim is that this nesting geometry might avoid the oscillatory locks that appear when recursive systems repeatedly truncate against simple rationals—not that φ is a law of nature.

On the primary comparison, systems implementing φ-based nesting maintained objective coherence longer than the control groups. After fifty recursive iterations, φ-systems retained approximately eighty-seven percent fidelity to their original specifications, compared with thirty-four percent for unstructured systems and fifty-two percent for simple rational scaling. The mechanism appeared related to resonance avoidance: rational locks more often produced alternating contradictory policies, thrashing between optimization states, and false “completion” signals, while φ-based systems showed smoother convergence with fewer pathological attractors.

The qualification is as important as the headline. Success varied by domain. Text generation showed the strongest φ benefit; policy optimization and parameter tuning showed moderate improvement; numerical optimization showed little difference between approaches. Longest continuous runs reached about two hundred iterations under computational constraints, so persistence across thousands of iterations remains unverified. Some systems improved early and degraded later, which means a mathematical invariant alone is not an indefinite warranty. Suite integrity here means the arms, metrics, and checkpoints held; it is not a universal crown for every recursive architecture.

Why a mixed fidelity result still helps

Methodologically, the work separates two questions builders often blur. First: can you measure fidelity decay under self-rewrite with arms fair enough to compare? Second: under that harness, does a particular nesting constant move the curve? The answer to the first is yes for these fixtures. The answer to the second is partial: φ beat unstructured and simple-rational arms on mean retention after fifty iterations, yet domain dependence and later degradation keep the strong hypothesis bounded.

That mixed read is what a senior test engineer should want when paychecks, callbacks, and household budgets ride on tools that rewrite tools. It prevents “just let it retrain” from winning by default when the expensive failure is loss of meaning. It also prevents a favorite constant from being mistaken for an AGI breakthrough or a finished peer-reviewed crown. Architectural constraints—meta-rules that survive self-modification—matter as much as any single algorithm. φ is one tested example of such a meta-principle; other constants may serve different designs.
