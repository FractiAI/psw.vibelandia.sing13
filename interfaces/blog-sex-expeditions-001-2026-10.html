When Biology and Experience Both Matter, Can a Combined Model Predict Better Than Either Alone?

FractiAI Research — October 23, 2026

People argue about sex the way households argue about money: everyone has a story, few people share the same ledger, and the stakes land in ordinary life—who gets trusted at work, who gets a second date, who carries unpaid care when a child is sick, who sees their preferences as “natural” and who sees them as learned. Sex Expeditions opens a research trail on that contested ground. The first paper asks a narrow, falsifiable question inside a larger program: in a controlled synthetic laboratory, does a model that uses both a biological predictor and an experiential predictor outperform models that use only one—and when does an interaction term help?

The problem the expedition refuses to flatten

Public debate often collapses into slogans. One side treats every sex-associated pattern as fixed biology. Another treats every pattern as culture all the way down. Neither posture is a research method. Couples still negotiate sleep, desire, and chores. Clinics still distinguish chromosomes, hormones, anatomy, and lived identity because those are different measurements. Employers still watch attrition when family leave and flexible hours interact with who is expected to “be flexible.” The scientific task is not to pick a team. It is to separate claims that need different evidence: an observable average difference, a biological contribution, an experiential contribution, a predictive association, and a beneficial complementarity under stated conditions.

Sex Expeditions places that work inside a larger recursive framing—Perpetual Recursive Self-Improvement within a Fractal-Holographic Goldilocks Super Intelligent Omniversal Universe catalog—without treating the framing as finished physics. The series asks how sex operates as a major recursive branch of biological organization, attraction, reproduction, relationships, and feedback across scales. Paper 001 is the first footpath, not the summit map.

What the synthetic laboratory did

The computational suite generated 28,000 synthetic observations: 4,000 simulated individuals in each of seven deliberately constructed scenarios. Two inputs stood in for hypothetical biological and experiential influences. Outcomes were built from known combinations of those inputs plus noise. Four ordinary least-squares models competed on a held-out 30% test set with a fixed seed of 20261023: biology-only, experience-only, combined additive, and combined plus interaction.

The scenarios were not metaphors for particular human traits. They were stress tests of the method: biology-dominant, experience-dominant, co-tuned, weak signals under high noise, balanced predictors with a true interaction, and two single-cause generators where the second predictor was irrelevant. That design makes a strong honesty demand: because the data-generating process was known in advance, success means the analysis can recover known structure—not that it discovered how real people work.

Hypothesis under test

The working hypothesis for the lab was trait-specific and modest. If both influences contribute, a combined additive model should beat single-predictor baselines on average. If a true interaction exists, adding the product term should raise held-out R². If noise dominates, even combined models should stay weak. If only one cause is real, extra predictors should not magically help. Those expectations map to research hypotheses H13–H15 in the paper. They do not settle which human preferences are biological, experiential, or both.

Results with qualifications

On the locked seed, mean held-out R² across the seven scenarios was about 0.400 for biology-only models, 0.382 for experience-only models, 0.768 for the combined additive model, and 0.785 when the interaction term was included. Dominant-cause scenarios behaved as designed: biology-only R² reached about 0.914 in the biology-dominant case and about 0.913 for experience-only in the experience-dominant case. In the balanced-with-interaction scenario, additive R² of 0.800 rose to 0.926 once the interaction entered the model. Under weak signals and high noise, combined performance stayed near 0.16. Where only one predictor generated the outcome, adding the irrelevant predictor did not materially improve the test score.

Those numbers are method results. They are not effect sizes for attraction, relationship satisfaction, workplace outcomes, or any surveyed attitude. Slightly negative R² for an irrelevant predictor simply means that model did worse than predicting the test-set mean—an expected failure mode, not a paradox about people.

What is still pending—and why that matters for rent and relationships

Paper 001 also names three real-data experiments that have not yet been run: cross-cultural attitudes in the WVS/EVS joint file, longitudinal relationship and stress dynamics in HARP, and developmental experience in Add Health. Documented sample sizes and repository pages prove the resources exist. They do not prove results. Until those analyses ship with weights, missingness, held-out validation, and uncertainty, no claim about cultural recurrence, couple-level change, or developmental prediction should be read into this note.

That restraint is practical, not decorative. Policy fights and dinner-table fights alike over-claim from incomplete ledgers. A parent negotiating custody schedules, a young adult sorting attraction from peer pressure, or a researcher comparing countries on family-role questions all need the same discipline: separate the measurement, report the overlap, and keep consent and dignity above any system-level story about “balance.”

Framework value without a victory lap

The integrated program’s proposed contribution is organizational: hold biological foundations, experience-layered tuning, co-tuning, complementarity, convergence-node metaphors, and net-zero downstream accounting in one research agenda while grading evidence instead of discarding meaningful averages because exceptions exist. Novelty and superiority against established models remain unproven. The synthetic lab shows a reusable comparison procedure. The real-data roadmap shows where that procedure must next meet actual observations.
