Can Tiny Gateway Organize Language and High-Rate Fusion Without Pretending It Already Won?

FractiAI Research — November 3, 2026

A parent parsing a school-portal message, a laid-off analyst learning a new job board, a wildlife ecologist watching collar tracks across a winter range—each is asking a practical question about reusable structure. Can you keep a pathway that already understands the shape of the work, bind fresh data into it, and execute without rebuilding the whole machine every Monday? Tiny Gateway Expedition 001 treats that question as a lab program, not a slogan. The stack under test is FractiAI’s named path—Boundary Node, Recursive Tree, golden-spiral scale bridge, Honeycomb Lattice, Wave–Node–Fq selection, template binding, and execution—and the assignment is to measure it against conventional small language pipelines and conventional multi-sensor fusion without assuming it is superior.

The problem the expedition names

Modern stacks waste money and attention in two familiar ways. Language tools either retrain heavy models for every narrow skill or glue brittle rules onto prompts until nobody can say which part actually decided the answer. Fusion pipelines either invent scoreboards on fantasy feeds or quote timing numbers that never lived in a preserved script. Paychecks and rent punish both habits. The expedition therefore refuses two temptations at once: do not quietly replace the FractiAI architecture with ordinary graph code and still call it Tiny Gateway, and do not treat previously reported, conflicting timing and memory figures as verified truth. If the famous six-sensor, one-hundred-eighty-track, eighty-step, eighty-six-thousand-four-hundred-observation civilian airspace brief is required, recreate it as a synthetic civilian fixture with fixed seeds—and say so. If Movebank GPS and geomagnetic Kp history are available, report them as a secondary arm, not as a costume for air-traffic control.

Curiosity doors stay open only when the scoreboard stays readable. Kids sorting recycling already know that a labeled system can match a hasty pile without beating it on speed. Adults comparing cover-letter tools know that “it understood my intent” is not the same claim as “it replaces the model that writes sentences.” The expedition keeps those distinctions loud.

What was built

The research suite implements every named stage as runnable code. Boundary nodes declare domain and entry. Recursive trees hold nested context. A golden-spiral scale bridge maps depth with the catalog’s Φ nesting ratio—an organizational bridge for filing scale, not a physics costume. Honeycomb lattices carry hex pathways. Wave–node–frequency activation scores templates and cues execution. Template binding separates reusable structure from incoming data. Ablation switches can disable spiral, honeycomb, wave, or tree so the suite can ask which pieces move runtime.

Beside Tiny Gateway sit two language arms: a lightweight TF-IDF and rule pipeline, and a pure JavaScript micro-Transformer with two layers. Neither is a commercial large language model. Keeping that size honest keeps the experiment about organization rather than about marketing a clinic-grade chatbot. For fusion, a nearest-neighbor association baseline runs beside the gateway arm on the same observations. Template stages—construction, selection, binding, execution, warm reuse, incremental update, cross-dataset reuse—are timed independently so a fast select cannot be smuggled in as a fast end-to-end win.

Hypothesis

If Tiny Gateway is more than filing theater, four things should show on locked seeds. First, language templates should organize bounded tasks at least as well as the lightweight baseline on a shared real-text set drawn from the prime-vault corpus and Hero Leo ledger. Second, fusion on the assignment civilian synthetic fixture should remain comparable to the conventional tracker on precision, recall, and F1. Third, separating construction, binding, and execution should yield warm reuse that is cheaper end-to-end than rebuilding ordinary closures—because that reuse claim is the architectural heart of the brief. Fourth, ablations should move runtime if the named components are doing real work. Engine pinning was withheld by design for this expedition series; gates are still reported so a later Player 1 decision can read measured evidence rather than hope.

What the live receipts show

Under seed twenty twenty-six eleven zero four, experiments E1 through E8 passed eight of eight as a protocol. Language source mode was historical real corpus plus capability fillers. On fifty-three evaluation rows, Tiny Gateway accuracy landed near zero point five eight five. The lightweight arm and the micro-Transformer both landed near zero point three seven seven. Training the gateway templates took a few milliseconds; training the micro-Transformer took tens. Four architectural role probes—language pathway, execution layer around a stub language model, partial replacement of neural intent routing, and a second specialized agent template—all returned okay. That is a bounded win for organization on real text. It still leaves cover-letter drafting for laid-off workers to other tools, and paraphrase recognition on unfamiliar sentences remains uneven across arms.

Fusion primary source mode was the assignment civilian synthetic fixture: six sensors, one hundred eighty tracks, eighty time steps, eighty-six thousand four hundred observations, eighteen injected anomaly tracks. Conventional F1 and Tiny Gateway F1 both measured about zero point zero nine seven—delta zero. The gateway paid a modest runtime premium on the full window, roughly four hundred thirteen milliseconds against about three hundred fifty-three for the conventional arm, a ratio near one point one seven. Scaling sweeps across sensor counts, track counts, and step budgets kept F1 tied between arms. When Movebank GPS plus Kp history was available as a secondary arm, both pipelines again tied near zero point two six one F1. The absolute synthetic F1 is modest because anomaly labels are heuristic; the point of the run is agreement and scaling under fixed seeds, not airspace authority for controllers.

Template binding told the sharpest negative. Selection, bind, and execute can each look cheap in isolation—select near a microsecond, bind and execute even lighter. Warm end-to-end reuse, which includes wave–node selection and honeycomb pathway work, sat near three microseconds median. A bare conventional rebuild sat near two-tenths of a microsecond. The reuse-versus-rebuild gate therefore failed. Selection alone can look fast; end-to-end compute is what rent-day engineering has to pay. Ablations on a smaller synthetic window moved runtime—disabling honeycomb or spiral cut cost—while F1 stayed flat, which is itself an honest finding: those components cost cycles without changing detection quality on this fixture.

Why a mixed result still earns an expedition

Architectural novelty and experimentally demonstrated performance are different currencies. Tiny Gateway Expedition 001 demonstrates a named, ablatable stack, a three-way language comparison, a recovered assignment-scale civilian fusion fixture, stage-isolated template accounting, and a refusal to crown an engine pin when warm reuse loses. That is enough to justify further development aimed at overhead, not enough to declare a reusable substrate for general intelligence. Conventional pipelines remain superior where bare closures and raw association speed matter. Tiny Gateway remains interesting where bounded language organization and explicit template roles matter.

The framework value for readers who keep kids’ schedules, job applications, and research notebooks in the same week is simple: ask for stage receipts. If someone sells “reusable templates,” ask for construct time, select time, bind time, execute time, and warm end-to-end time against an optimized conventional rebuild. If someone sells “radar fusion,” ask whether the feed is synthetic, historical wildlife GPS, or actual authorized aviation data—and never let those three blur. Curiosity doors stay safer when the labels stay sharp.
