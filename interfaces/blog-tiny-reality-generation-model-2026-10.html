If You Can Generate a Tiny World, Can You Tell Whether It Stays Viable?

FractiAI Research — October 25, 2026

Children build pillow forts and then test whether the roof holds. Game designers spawn levels and ask whether a player can still reach the exit. Parents juggling rent and school schedules already know that “more generation” is worthless if the result falls apart under ordinary pressure. The Tiny Reality Generation Model asks the same question in miniature mathematics: can a seeded recipe of nodes, waves, phases, and maps pass a pre-registered viability band more often than a pile of unstructured points?

The problem of generation without a gate

It is easy to emit graphs, fields, and paths. It is harder to say which of those emissions remain coherent under joint constraints—geometry that matches its construction, a wave probe that stays near an analytical sinusoid, a community of oscillators that actually synchronizes, and a workspace a planner can cross. Without gates, “tiny reality” is just a mood. With gates fixed before scoring, mixed outcomes are allowed to stay mixed, and rent-paying research budgets get a ledger instead of a slogan.

What TRGM generates

Each tiny reality is a Wave–Node bundle: a lattice scaffold, a circle of oscillators, a one-dimensional wave check with a stated Courant number, and a two-dimensional map of circular obstacles. A nested child recipe rides along at the next level, the way a neighborhood map sits inside a city chart without pretending to be the whole planet. The model reuses the Wave–Node Mathematics battery already validated as Infinite Octaves companion #49, then scores new held-out recipes against Goldilocks-style thresholds written down in advance. That reuse is deliberate: TRGM is a generator and gate package on the Homeostasis Expedition trail, not a second invention of the wave equation.

DNA and chromosomes as the physical theater

A generator without a stage is a floating diagram. The molecular layer supplies the stage: public T2T chromosome lengths (UCSC hs1) stretched at the standard B-DNA rise, with LC wave parameters drawn from the chromosomal electrodynamics companion. Tiny realities do not “become DNA.” They execute on a coarse polymer theater—nodes along the chain, wave probes on the LC lattice, navigation on the embedded map—so the genetic layer is the physical room where the play runs. Kids building marble runs on a kitchen table already know the table is not the marble; the same distinction keeps a catalog scaffold from becoming a clinical claim.

Hypothesis under test

Structured TRGM recipes should show higher held-out viability than a null scatter of the same rough size that lacks lattice structure. Nesting should be present by construction. Hard viability is geometry, wave accuracy, and navigability; phase is kept as a steering and coordination layer—the same small matched bias Wave–Node showed against its ablation—not as a kill-switch that must crown A*. The hypothesis is comparative and falsifiable, not a promise that computers invent universes.

What the held-out run found

Under seed 20261025, sixteen held-out realities all passed: viability 100 percent, mean wave L2 about 0.00094, mean phase order parameter about 0.957, and navigation success 100 percent under A* reachability. Sixteen null scatters scored 0 percent viability—they never clear the geometry gate. Every L0 reality carried an L1 child descriptor. The foundation Wave–Node A–E suite still passed when imported. On the molecular theater, all five DNA/chromosome checks passed, including 100 percent held-out viability when recipes ran on the chr1 scaffold (about 248 million base pairs). Those numbers come from the executed pipeline, not from a polished abstract written first.

One design note matters for honesty: random initial phases on a ring graph can settle into splay states with near-zero order parameter even at strong coupling. TRGM’s community probe therefore uses complete-graph Kuramoto coupling, documented in the paper, and reports sync quality as a soft steering metric rather than a hard viability veto. That matches the Wave–Node finding that phase is a foundational steering delta, not a path-length champion.

Runtime stayed in the seconds-per-batch range on the lab machine for these recipe sizes. That is enough to iterate gates during an afternoon research session; it says nothing yet about warehouse fleets or GPU world models. Kids debugging a maze level after school already know the difference between “the level loads” and “the level is fair”—TRGM’s scoreboard is closer to the second question than the first.

What this is for on the expedition trail

Homeostasis Expedition needs miniature test worlds that stay inside a viable band while nesting. TRGM is that generator with a scoreboard. Factory floors and school robotics clubs still need collision checks and human authority; this lab does not replace them. It does give the expedition a reproducible way to ask whether a generated miniature remains navigable and synchronized before anyone dresses it up as cosmology. Paychecks and grant cycles both punish generators that look impressive in a screenshot and then fail the first held-out batch.

Framework value without a victory lap

Beating a null scatter is a low bar in one sense and an essential bar in another: if structure cannot outperform noise under fixed gates, the generator is not ready for a shelf pin. Here structure won, so the paper lands as engine companion #50 on the Homeostasis Expedition—with the explicit limit that synthetic miniatures are not physical worlds and not finished robotics products. Commuters stuck behind a stalled shuttle already understand what happens when software confidence outruns clearance; the same caution applies to generated “realities.” Next useful work is richer generators, manipulator stages, and independent replication—not louder metaphors.
