## Abstract

This paper establishes **SEX EXPEDITIONS — Expedition 001 · Paper 001**, investigating sex as a major recursive branch of Perpetual Recursive Self-Improvement within the Fractal-Holographic Goldilocks Super Intelligent Omniversal Universe.

The expedition examines biological reproduction, sex-associated traits, differentiation, attraction, preferences, behaviors, cycles, experience-layered tuning, complementarity, and the potential roles of humans within interconnected systems. Its central biological-versus-experiential research objective asks which traits have identifiable biological foundations, which are influenced by experience and environment, and which emerge through their interaction.

An initial computational laboratory experiment used **28,000 synthetic observations** across **seven constructed scenarios** (seed `20261023`). Combined predictive models performed better on average than single-predictor models in this simulation (mean held-out $R^2$ **0.768** combined additive vs **0.400** biology-only and **0.382** experience-only), while interaction-aware modeling improved prediction in a scenario deliberately generated with an interaction ($R^2$ **0.800** additive → **0.926** with interaction). These results demonstrate a **method**, not discoveries about actual human sex differences.

The paper incorporates a real-data experimental program using publicly documented resources: the World Values Survey/European Values Study (WVS/EVS), the Health and Relationships Project (HARP), and Add Health. These datasets offer complementary opportunities to examine cross-cultural variation, relationship dynamics, and developmental experience. **The raw-data analyses remain pending; no new results from those datasets are claimed here.**

The broader integrated framework’s novelty, generality, and real-world predictive value remain to be established through systematic literature review, empirical testing, and comparison with established models.

## 3. Biological Foundations and Experience-Layered Tuning

Determine which sex-associated traits, behaviors, preferences, attractions, and cycles have biological foundations, which are shaped by experience and environment, and which emerge through their interaction.

For each trait, distinguish among:

- **Biological foundation:** Genetic, anatomical, physiological, hormonal, or developmental contributions.
- **Experience-layered tuning:** Contributions from learning, relationships, rewards, disappointments, culture, and social circumstances.
- **Co-tuning:** Interactions in which biological conditions and experience jointly influence the outcome.
- **Unresolved:** Evidence insufficient to determine the contributions reliably.

“Hardcoded” is a working metaphor, not a presumption of immutability. A biological contribution does not establish that a trait is fixed, exclusive to one sex, or inevitable.

### 3.1 Core Natural Tuning Hypothesis

Attraction, taste, preferences, behavioral tendencies, and priorities may emerge from interacting biological predispositions, development, experience, culture, and current circumstances.

The proposed recursive model is:

**Biological foundations + development + experience + environment → individual tuning → attention and preferences → choices and interactions → outcomes and feedback → updated tuning.**

This is a model to test, not an established universal mechanism.

Cycles must be investigated individually. Menstrual and other reproductive cycles have established biological mechanisms; other cycles involving attraction, attention, or preferences require separate evidence.

### 3.2 Trait-by-trait research strategy

For each selected trait:

1. Define what is being measured.
2. Review existing evidence and proposed mechanisms.
3. Distinguish correlation from causation.
4. Evaluate biological, experiential, and interaction-based explanations.
5. Examine differences across individuals, groups, populations, and contexts.
6. Quantify effect sizes, uncertainty, overlap, and predictive value.
7. Compare competing models and preserve unresolved questions.

The objective is to determine the balance of influences trait by trait rather than presume a universal answer.

## 4. Comparative Investigation Across Species

Research should compare relevant patterns across humans and other species, distinguishing:

- Reproductive biology and sex-associated physiological traits.
- Behavioral and social patterns.
- Similarities, differences, and variation within groups.
- Biological mechanisms versus environmental and developmental influences.
- Findings that generalize across species from those that do not.

Animal evidence can generate hypotheses about humans, but it does not by itself establish human mechanisms or prescribe human behavior. Similar-looking behaviors can arise through different mechanisms, and similar mechanisms can produce different behaviors under different conditions.

Biological sex, gender identity, gender roles, and culturally defined masculinity and femininity must be measured separately rather than treated as interchangeable.

## 5. Differences, Complementarity, and Natural Tuning

The research will identify predominant patterns where supported, without requiring every individual to fit them.

Complementarity is a testable possibility: different traits or capabilities may work well together under particular conditions. Differences do not automatically produce complementarity, and complementarity does not require every partnership or group to include different sexes.

The investigation should determine whether specific combinations improve defined outcomes relative to realistic alternatives, accounting for coordination costs, individual preferences, consent, and context.

The research should distinguish a difference that is observable, a difference that has a biological contribution, a difference shaped by experience, a difference that predicts an outcome, and a difference that contributes to a beneficial interaction. These are separate claims requiring different evidence.

---

## 6. Evidence Principle: Preserve Meaningful Variation

Findings do not need to apply to every individual to be useful. A reliable average difference, subgroup pattern, or context-dependent effect should not be discarded merely because exceptions exist.

For each finding, record effect size, uncertainty, group overlap, variation, context, replication, and practical significance. Small effects may matter when reliable or cumulative; large effects may have limited value when unstable or poorly measured.

Extensive similarities do not erase meaningful differences, and meaningful differences do not establish universal opposites.

Weak evidence receives limited weight—not automatic acceptance or automatic dismissal. Contradicted claims should be revised or rejected. Variation should be investigated as a potential clue to underlying mechanisms rather than automatically treated as noise.

## 7. Computational Laboratory: Biological and Experiential Contributions

### 7.1 Research question

Can a computational experiment distinguish simulated biological contributions from experience-layered contributions, identify interactions, and determine when a combined model improves prediction?

### 7.2 Experimental design

The synthetic laboratory generated **28,000 observations**: **4,000** simulated individuals for each of **seven** deliberately constructed trait scenarios.

Two input variables represented hypothetical biological and experiential influences. Outcomes were generated using predefined combinations of these predictors, with controlled random noise.

The scenarios represented biology-dominant influence, experience-dominant influence, combined influence, weak signals under substantial noise, balanced influences with an added interaction, and two cases where only one predictor generated the outcome.

Four regression models were compared:

| Model | Predictors |
|-------|------------|
| Biology-only | Biological predictor |
| Experience-only | Experiential predictor |
| Combined additive | Both predictors |
| Combined plus interaction | Both predictors and their product |

Each scenario used a **70%** training and **30%** held-out test split. Models were evaluated using test-set $R^2$, which measures predictive performance relative to predicting the test-set mean. The random seed was fixed at **20261023**. Pipeline: `npm run research:synthobs-sex-expeditions-001`.

### 7.3 Results

Held-out test $R^2$ under seed `20261023` (synthetic only):

| Synthetic scenario | Biology-only | Experience-only | Combined additive | Combined + interaction |
|--------------------|-------------:|----------------:|------------------:|-----------------------:|
| Biology-dominant | 0.914 | 0.041 | 0.952 | 0.951 |
| Experience-dominant | 0.055 | 0.913 | 0.952 | 0.952 |
| Co-tuned | 0.515 | 0.536 | 0.944 | 0.944 |
| Weak signals, high noise | 0.103 | 0.051 | 0.157 | 0.156 |
| Balanced with interaction | 0.447 | 0.347 | 0.800 | 0.926 |
| Biology-only generator | 0.776 | −0.003 | 0.776 | 0.776 |
| Experience-only generator | −0.009 | 0.793 | 0.793 | 0.792 |
| **Mean across scenarios** | **0.400** | **0.382** | **0.768** | **0.785** |

All values are from the synthetic simulation, not estimates of actual human traits. Slightly negative $R^2$ on irrelevant predictors means the model underperformed predicting the test-set mean—expected when the predictor is unrelated to the outcome.

### 7.4 Findings

1. **Combined predictors can improve prediction.** Mean test $R^2$ **0.768** (combined additive) vs **0.400** (biology-only) and **0.382** (experience-only).
2. **The dominant influence varies by trait.** Biology-only prediction performed strongly in the biology-dominant scenario; experience-only prediction performed strongly in the experience-dominant scenario.
3. **Interactions can matter.** In the balanced-with-interaction scenario, additive $R^2$ **0.800** increased to **0.926** with the interaction term.
4. **Noise limits prediction.** In the weak-signal, high-noise scenario, combined $R^2$ ≈ **0.16**.
5. **More complexity is not always better.** Where an outcome was generated by only one predictor, adding the irrelevant predictor did not materially improve test-set performance.

### 7.5 Interpretation and limits

The experiment demonstrates that this analytical procedure can distinguish known simulated patterns under the conditions tested. It does **not** establish which human traits are biologically grounded, which observed sex differences are caused by experience, or what proportions of real behavior are explained by either factor.

Because the data-generating mechanisms were specified in advance, this was a methodological demonstration rather than an independent discovery about humans. Applying the framework to actual populations requires appropriate empirical data, careful measurement, consideration of confounding factors, and independent validation.

## 8. Real-Data Experimental Program

The next stage extends the computational laboratory to real observations. Three established data resources have been identified, each addressing a different part of the research program.

**Important status distinction:** These are **specified** real-data experiments, **not completed analyses**. Repository documentation establishes that the datasets exist and describes their coverage; it does not establish results for this paper. **No coefficients, effect sizes, or predictive scores from their raw observations are claimed here.**

### 8.1 Experiment A — Cross-cultural patterns in sex-associated attitudes

**Dataset:** World Values Survey / European Values Study joint dataset, 2017–2022.

The joint dataset documentation reports 156,658 cases across 92 countries and territories, with 231 variables in the joint file (EVS/WVS, 2022).

**Research question:** Which measured attitudes relevant to family, relationships, gender roles, and social values show recurring differences by respondent sex, and how much do those patterns vary across countries, age groups, and social contexts?

**Proposed procedure:** Obtain joint dataset, questionnaires, and codebook; identify relevant attitude variables plus respondent sex, age, education, country, and covariates; inspect response categories, missingness, sampling design, and weights; estimate weighted descriptive patterns and sex-associated differences; test variation across countries and age groups; report effect sizes, CIs, $n$, and within-group variation; validate predictive models on held-out observations with country-aware splits where appropriate.

**Limitations:** Survey attitudes ≠ direct biological mechanisms or all behaviors. Country differences may reflect many historical and institutional factors. Cross-sectional associations do not establish causation.

### 8.2 Experiment B — Relationship dynamics, stress, and change over time

**Dataset:** Health and Relationships Project (HARP), United States, 2014–2025 (ICPSR 37404).

HARP follows spouses in same-sex and different-sex marriages with baseline questionnaires and 10-day daily diaries across waves covering stress, health, social interactions, and relationship quality.

**Research question:** How do individual characteristics, relationship context, stress, and daily experiences relate to relationship and health outcomes over time? Do relevant associations differ across the sampled relationship groups?

**Limitations:** Not a representative sample of all people or relationship types. Longitudinal associations alone do not prove causality. Group comparisons must account for sampling, power, and participant circumstances.

### 8.3 Experiment C — Development and experience-layered tuning

**Dataset:** Add Health (National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health).

Add Health includes longitudinal data on social, psychological, physical, family, friendship, and romantic-relationship experiences. Access depends on the specific dataset and variables; some information is restricted.

**Research question:** To what extent do early social experiences, family context, peer relationships, and developmental circumstances predict later measured relationship attitudes or outcomes, after accounting for relevant baseline characteristics?

**Limitations:** Developmental experience is not randomly assigned. Unmeasured confounding, recall error, attrition, and changing measurements can affect results.

### 8.4 Experiment D — Cross-dataset synthesis

Following individual analyses, compare findings at the level of compatible research questions—not by assuming different questionnaires measure the same trait.

**Research question:** Which patterns are replicated across datasets, which depend on context or measurement, and does combining biological proxies, developmental variables, and environmental measures improve prediction where comparable measures exist?

## 9. Real-Data Analysis Standards

All real-data experiments should follow these minimum standards:

1. Use official datasets, questionnaires, codebooks, and data citations.
2. Define outcomes, predictors, exclusions, and hypotheses before inspecting final results where feasible.
3. Account for survey weights, clustering, repeated measurements, and paired observations when relevant.
4. Report sample sizes, missingness, effect sizes, uncertainty intervals, and model assumptions.
5. Compare against simple baselines rather than presenting a complex model in isolation.
6. Separate predictive performance from causal evidence.
7. Validate on held-out data and avoid leakage between training and test samples.
8. Preserve meaningful subgroup patterns while disclosing uncertainty and limitations.
9. Avoid treating respondent sex as a complete proxy for biology, identity, gender roles, or experience.
10. Record code, dataset versions, and analysis decisions to make results reproducible.

If supported, the framework could clarify how biological and experiential influences shape attraction and preferences, identify context-dependent patterns, and improve understanding of relationships and coordination. ## 14. Limitations and Ethical Requirements

The framework is exploratory. Correlation does not establish causation; group averages do not determine individual characteristics; and biological influence does not establish immutability.

The synthetic experiment used simplified, known data-generating mechanisms. Its scores depend on design choices, predictor distributions, noise levels, and model specifications. They cannot be interpreted as estimates of real-world performance.

The real-data program has been specified using documented datasets, but raw-data analyses remain pending. Dataset availability and documented sample sizes must not be mistaken for completed analyses. Access restrictions, survey design, measurement differences, missingness, and statistical power may constrain which questions can be answered.

Further research must avoid confounding sex with gender, overstating cross-species comparisons, selecting only confirming evidence, or treating proposed models as validated mechanisms. Findings should be replicated, uncertainty reported, and competing explanations considered.

Research involving people must protect informed consent, privacy, autonomy, dignity, and safety. Data use must comply with the terms and conditions of each repository.
